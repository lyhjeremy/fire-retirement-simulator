When can I retire?
FIRE simulator / US returns 1871 to 2022 / browser only

October 2026 upgrade: real market history

One plan, three models of the market: 82%, 85% and 93%

The simulator used to draw every year's return from a bell curve. It now runs on 152 years of US stock and bond returns built from Robert Shiller's data, and can replay a plan through every retirement year in the record, resample that record in blocks, or keep the bell curve for comparison. The default plan (age 32, $150k saved, $40k a year saved, $60k a year spent from 50 to 95, 75% stocks) lasts in 82% of normal draws, 85% of bootstrap paths and 84 of 90 historical retirement years.

Test log

What moved when the bell curve went

The old app's default assumed a 5.0% real return with 13.0% volatility for the whole portfolio and gave the same plan 56% in a run recorded in the README (54% with the seed used here). Most of the jump to the new figures comes from the return assumption, not the model: US stocks returned 6.9% a year after inflation from 1871 to 2022 (8.4% arithmetic mean, 17.9% standard deviation), and a 75/25 mix with 10-year Treasuries comes out well above 5%. Holding the mean and spread fixed and changing only how years are drawn moves the result from 82% to 93%.

Historical start years

1929 and 1966 survive; 1973 to 1977 do not

The historical engine lines the whole life up with history: "retire in 1929" means saving from 1911 to 1928 and drawing down from 1929. For a 30-year retiree with a fixed pot, 1929 and 1966 are the classic failures. For this plan they are not, because the decades before them built large balances: the 1966 retiree arrives with $2.77 million after saving through 1948 to 1965, when the 75/25 mix returned 10.7% a year after inflation. The 1973 to 1977 retirees saved through weaker years, reach retirement with $0.85 to $1.5 million, and then meet 6.6 to 8.7% a year inflation in their first decade. They run out between 83 and 95. The 1917 retiree runs out at 92.

Why history is kinder here

Long runs of years carry information that single draws throw away

The bootstrap draws runs of consecutive real years, stock and bond together. With one-year blocks it is close to the normal engine (82.6% against 82.2%), so fat tails alone do little to this plan. Longer blocks raise the success rate, and full historical replay raises it most. Over a 63-year plan, bad decades in US history have tended to be followed by better ones, and independent draws cannot reproduce that.

Holdout

Fitted on 1871 to 1945, both random engines were too hopeful about the post-war years

To see whether the random engines are calibrated, I fitted them on 1871 to 1945 only and asked them about a 30-year retirement on a 75/25 mix, then compared their answers with what actually happened to the 48 retirements that started from 1946 to 1993. At 4% withdrawals the bootstrap said 96% and the normal engine 93%; 92% of those retirements lasted. At 5% they said 88% and 81%, and 73% lasted. From 4% up, both engines overstated success in the post-war sample, and the bootstrap missed by more.

Benchmark

The 4% rule on this data lands a few points under the Trinity study

Before trusting the engine I ran the textbook case: $40,000 a year from $1 million, adjusted for inflation, for 30 years, replayed through every start year. The Trinity study (Cooley, Hubbard and Walz, 1998) reported 95% success at 50/50 and 98% at 75/25 for start years 1926 to 1966. Bengen (1994) found 4.15% the highest rate that survived every 30-year period with 50 to 75% stocks.

The safe maximum is the highest starting rate, to 0.01%, that lasted 30 years from every start year. Damodaran's calendar-year series (Dec 31 to Dec 31) gives the same failing years at 50/50 and 75/25, so January-to-January timing is not the cause.

This data fails about one more start year than Trinity at each mix: 1965 and 1966 at 75/25, 1964 to 1966 at 50/50. They run out late, in their 25th to 29th year (the 1965 retiree at 94, a year short), and the safe maximum comes out at 3.75% for 75/25 against Bengen's 4.15%. Both studies used different bonds from mine: Trinity long-term corporate bonds and Bengen intermediate Treasuries, where I use 10-year Treasuries derived from yields. I have not tested how much of the gap that explains.

Spending rules

Flexible spending buys success with lower spending in bad stretches

The app has three spending rules. Constant spending withdraws $60k every year. The percentage rule spends 4% of the portfolio, held between a floor of 75% and a ceiling of 150% of the target. Guardrails, after Guyton and Klinger, start at $60k and cut 10% when the withdrawal rate rises 20% above where it started (except in the last 15 years) or raise 10% when it falls 20% below, within the same floor and ceiling. A pension or Social Security payment reduces what the portfolio has to cover from the age it starts.

Solver

What gets the plan to 90%

For the plan on screen, the solver searches each lever on its own: the first later retirement age that reaches the target, the highest spending (to $500), the lowest yearly saving (to $500), and the fixed stock share closest to the current one. Under the bootstrap the default plan needs one of: retire at 52, spend $51,500, or save $48,500 a year. No fixed mix gets it there; 100% stocks reaches 88%. Under historical replay it already clears 90%, and the solver reports the slack instead: spending can rise to $62,500 or saving fall to $38,000. Normal and bootstrap searches reuse the same 5,000 seeded paths for every candidate, so the comparison is between plans, not between random draws, and the search runs in a Web Worker in under a second.

Method

Data and engine

scripts/build_returns.py reads Shiller's monthly ie_data.xls. Stock returns chain his monthly total return, (Pm+1 + Dm/12) / Pm, from January to January. Bond returns are a constant-maturity approximation: buy a 10-year bond at par at January's yield, collect a year's coupon, and sell it as a 9-year bond at the next January's yield. Before 1953 the yield column is Shiller's long-term government rate. Each series is deflated by the change in January CPI. Shiller's file runs to September 2023, with estimated CPI for its last two months, so the last full year is 2022.

Against Damodaran's histretSP.xls for 1928 to 2022, the series agree on average (real stocks 8.33% against 8.26% a year, real bonds 1.88% against 1.87%) and correlate at 0.97 year by year. Single years differ because Damodaran uses December 31 closes and Shiller monthly averages: 1987 is -6.2% here and +1.4% there, because a sharp rally in January 1987 put that month's average 6% above December's, so this window starts higher. The bond approximation matches Shiller's own monthly bond-return column to 0.34 points a year on average.

Data
Shiller, S&P Composite, CPI and GS10, 1871 to 2022 (152 years), real, January to January
Normal
Independent bivariate normal stock and bond draws with the data's means, standard deviations and correlation (0.19); stock mean and volatility adjustable
Bootstrap
Moving blocks of consecutive years with wraparound, stock and bond drawn together; block length 1 to 20, default 10
Historical
One window per retirement year; working years use the years just before it; windows that pass 2022 are shown but not counted
Timing
Savings added at year end while working; spending withdrawn at the start of each retirement year; rebalanced yearly
Checks
scripts/engine.py ports the engine to Python; scripts/check_engine.py --js runs both and the benchmarks
Stack
Static files, vanilla JavaScript and a Web Worker, no libraries, no backend, no analytics

Limitations

What this still leaves out

The data is one country's history, and the US had one of the best stock markets of the period. Every engine here inherits that, and the holdout shows the random engines were too optimistic about the post-war years even with US data.

There are no taxes, fees, one-off costs, or changes in spending with age. Bonds are a single 10-year Treasury series with no TIPS, cash or international assets. Contributions stay fixed in real terms through the working years. The historical engine for a long plan has few complete windows: 90 for the default plan, heavily overlapping.

Use it to compare plans against each other. It is an educational tool and does not give financial advice.
