When can I retire?
FIRE Monte Carlo simulator / browser only

Monte Carlo, 5,000 paths per run

The odds your savings last to 95, and the earliest age that gets them to 90%

You enter eight numbers: age, retirement age, savings, yearly contribution, yearly spending, plan-until age, expected real return and volatility. The simulator plays the plan forward a year at a time, 5,000 times, with each year's return drawn at random, and reports the share of paths that never run out of money. Moving the retirement age alone takes the default plan from almost certain failure to 90%.

Why simulate

The same returns in a different order end differently

The 4% rule and "save 25 times your spending" describe an average future. A crash in the first years of retirement does far more damage than the same crash twenty years later, because you are selling to live while prices are down. Thousands of random sequences put a number on that risk: the share of 5,000 paths that never touch zero before the plan-until age.

Method

What happens in one run

For each of 5,000 paths the simulator starts from current savings and steps forward a year at a time. Each year it draws a return from a normal distribution with your mean and standard deviation (a Box-Muller transform on Math.random), applies it to the balance, then adds the contribution if you are still working or subtracts spending if you have retired. A balance that goes negative is set to zero and the path is marked as failed.

After the loop it sorts the 5,000 balances at every age and reads off the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th and 90th percentiles for the chart. The earliest-age search tries every retirement age from next year upward at 2,500 paths each, stops at the first that reaches 90%, and reruns that age at 5,000 paths for the card you see.

Return draw
Normal, Box-Muller, mean and volatility set by the user (defaults 5.0% and 13.0% real)
Paths
5,000 per run; 2,500 per candidate age during the earliest-age search
Success
Share of paths whose balance stays above zero through the plan-until age
Chart
Percentile bands drawn on a <canvas>, no charting library; hover or arrow keys read any age

Limitations

What this model leaves out

Returns are independent draws from a normal distribution. Market returns have fatter tails, and their bad years cluster in a way independent draws can't reproduce, so the model is probably a little optimistic about the worst outcomes.

Return and spending are both constant in real terms. The model leaves out taxes, pensions and Social Security, a glide path between stocks and bonds, one-off costs, and cutting back in a bad year. The last is the biggest gap: people who have retired do adjust their spending, and that flexibility would raise the success rate.

Use it to compare plans against each other. It is an educational tool and does not give financial advice.
